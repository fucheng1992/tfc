Research

Research directions

My work moves from the mechanics and numerical description of cracks, to the physical laws governing their dynamics, and finally to principles for making soft materials tougher.

01 / Modeling and methods

Fracture mechanics

I develop computational frameworks for fracture at finite strain, where conventional formulations face severe costs, mesh distortion, volumetric locking, and a fundamental conflict between incompressibility and diffuse crack opening.

  • Adaptive phase-field methods. Hybrid triangular-quadrilateral meshes identify and follow crack tips, refine only the active fracture region, and coarsen the wake. This keeps the resolution required for branching cracks while reducing memory and computational cost.
  • Large strain and near incompressibility. Edge-based smoothed finite elements improve tolerance to distorted meshes. Mixed displacement-pressure-phase field formulations relax the incompressibility constraint only inside damaged material. A stabilized equal-order formulation further enables simple P1 interpolation and, together with energy-based adaptive refinement, accelerates computation by more than fifteen times.
  • Interfaces and nonlocal fracture. A hybrid ES-FEM/FEM framework resolves crack penetration and deflection at soft interfaces, while an extended ordinary state-based peridynamic model carries nonlinear deformation and fracture into a meshfree, nonlocal setting.

02 / Speed, path and instability

Fracture physics

I study what determines the speed, path, and stability of a moving crack. The central issue is local: nonlinear deformation and damage near the tip alter the wave field seen by the crack, producing branching, oscillation, arrest, or supershear propagation.

  • Limit velocity and bifurcation. Phase-field simulations of brittle fracture reveal an energy-flux criterion for crack bifurcation. Combining this criterion with continuum theory explains why branching can impose an upper limit on crack speed.
  • Wave speed inside a diffuse crack. Classical dynamic phase-field models can artificially degrade the local wave speed as damage grows, making the predicted limiting velocity depend on the model rather than the material. A wave-speed-invariant formulation reproduces the rapid oscillation and tip-splitting instabilities observed in brittle gels.
  • From sub-Rayleigh to supershear. Mode-II simulations identify a critical prestress and distinguish direct transitions from mother-daughter crack nucleation. In soft materials, strain softening favors branching and sub-Rayleigh motion, whereas strain stiffening raises the local wave speed, stabilizes the tip, and permits supershear fracture. Elastic strips further expose an intrinsic local scale controlling dynamic cracks.

03 / Architecture and dissipation

Toughening mechanisms

I seek minimal physical rules for overcoming the usual trade-off between strength and toughness. A three-dimensional soft-hard composite model strips away complex constitutive behavior and network geometry so that the essential role of component contrast and composition can be isolated.

  • A minimal origin of toughening. Random networks of linear-elastic soft and hard elements reproduce mechanical hysteresis, sacrificial-element dissipation, and Mullins-like responses. These effects therefore do not require elaborate nonlinear constitutive assumptions.
  • Brittle-to-ductile transition. At low soft-phase content, fracture remains a narrow localized damage band. Above a critical composition, widespread internal fracture of the hard phase dissipates energy and the composite becomes ductile. The transition follows a mechanical-equilibrium condition based on the failure stresses of the two components.
  • Defect screening and load transfer. In coupled hard-soft double networks, mechanical contrast transfers load away from damaged regions and screens stress concentrations. This connects microscopic defect shielding to macroscopic yielding, stable necking, and the transition from localized fracture to delocalized damage.
